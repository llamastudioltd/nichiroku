When the developers behind [Ruby on Rails][1] and [Merb][2], (two Ruby-based MVC frameworks similar in functionality if not ideology) announced the [merging the two projects][3], there was significant uproar from the communities of both frameworks. Both sets of developers were concerned that the software they used and often contributed to would die off or that they would have to massively change the way they worked. Instead this initial outcry quickly gave way to the realisation that the vast proportion of Ruby developers would now be using the same codebase and the benefits that this would bring. Features would be easier and faster to develop, test and build. Bugs would be discovered and fixed much more quickly. Larger groups of talented developers would be available to work on the project. The resultant framework, Ruby on Rails 3, is now progressing further, faster and without sacrificing the ideologies that either of its parents held.

The, admittedly much larger, community of PHP developers is following a very different and more self-destructive path. For every disagreement there is a fork or a new project, even for something as trivial as the implementation of libraries or the support for a particular version. Developers are happier to fragment their potential audience by endlessly forking projects instead of trying to accomplish the much harder task of overcoming differences and working together.  

In the case of Ruby on Rails and Merb, the significant cult of personality surrounding [David Heinemeier Hansson][4] and the opinionated underpinnings of both frameworks were huge obstacles that were successfully overcome. The PHP community doesn't have a driving force behind it that is nearly as resistant to change. [Ellislab][5], the creators of [CodeIgniter][6] and possibly the closest example in the PHP world, took the sensible step of [relinquishing direct control][7] in order to push their software forward at a faster pace. Despite this lack of obstacles and the willingness of key players to promote better collaboration, there is still this broken culture of taking your ball and playing by yourself.

In a conversation with [Phil Sturgeon][8], a PHP developer and contributor to both CodeIgniter and [Fuel][9], he argued that the proliferation of new frameworks is good news as the choice allows people to find a way that works best for them. He states that if you don't like how something works, its more constructive to build anew. Where this argument fails is that having a huge choice makes it harder to identify frameworks that are stable, consistent, well built and suitable for a purpose. I've been using CodeIgniter since it was first released and in that time I've used it to build websites that have a combined audience of well into the millions. Frequently I've looked at competing frameworks and it is always hard for me to best decide what to migrate to. I know that CodeIgniter is missing core features but I also have no way of knowing how other frameworks will perform or for how long they will exist.

If more people use a framework, the better it will become. There will be a greater number of more intelligent people planning, developing and documenting features. There will be more copies deployed, there will be more bugs discovered and more security vulnerabilities patched. Instead of exponentially splitting the potential contributor and user base, the product will become exponentially better. Of course, people will have to adapt to a mindset that may differ from theirs. PHP has been in existence for far longer than Ruby as a web platform and so there are many more established conventions and opinions. But without people adapting, there will never be the collaboration that creates great software.
