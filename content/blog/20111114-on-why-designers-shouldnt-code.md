An enduring argument in design circles has come around once more, this time in the form of an [opinionated post][1] from [Andy Rutledge][2]. He states that there is a huge gap between designers <a href="#Note-1" id="Link-1">&sup1;</a> who have sufficient knowledge to build websites and those that do not. 

> A designer who does not write markup and CSS is not designing for the web, but drawing pictures.

Andy isn't the first person to hold this view and certainly won't be the last. Many [designers][3] [and][4] [developers][5] have also given their reasons for believing that the skills required to build software are as important as those needed to design it. I'd like to argue that they are approaching this argument from the perspective of their particular skill-set and from a misplaced order of priorities.

The primary aim when building software should be to solve the problems of a particular set of users. This aim above all others should govern what you are looking to create and how it should be created. A project focussed on the user experience will first concentrate on discovering exactly what a user's needs are and only once these have been identified can you begin to generate solutions.

During this discovery phase there should be little idea of what the solution will be yet alone what it will look like or in what language it will be built. With the stipulation that designers should be well versed in how to develop for a platform you are limiting yourself <a href="#Note-2" id="Link-2">&sup2;</a> in terms of the solutions available to you. Your designers will be more inclined to work with what they are comfortable with and less inclined to consider alternatives, even if these alternatives would be a better fit in providing for the user needs.

As the project progresses these designers will be constantly shifting their focus away from the identified goals of your project and on to how their ideas might work when they are being developed. A designer should have enough of an understanding of the medium to know what is possible, in much the same way as an architect can select materials without knowing how they are manufactured, but a designer should not be concerned with how difficult something may be to implement as they will be less likely to consider it, even if that is to the detriment of the user.

It's ironic that it was Andy Rutledge who resurrected this argument as it helps to better convey my point. Recently he [presented his opinions][6] on how badly designed the [New York Times website][7] is. His solution was based around stripping the website of a large number of features vital to the business or the user but which he felt unnecessary. Unfortunately his proposal was widely criticised for ignoring or massively misunderstanding the site's intended users and their needs. He entirely ignored the most important aspect of design, the user experience, and instead focused only on his knowledge of one particular medium and his ability to work within it.

In short, if you are a designer who is overly concerned with the 'how’ of what you are designing rather than the 'why’ you are concerned more with the parts of the project that are important to you than those important to the user, and that results in a product that is less useful to both of you. 

- <a href="#Link-1" id="Note-1">&sup1;</a> For the sake of this argument I'm going to assume the term designer to mean graphic, interaction, UX and all other disciplines involved in the designing of websites and other software.
- <a href="#Link-2" id="Note-2">&sup2;</a> Unless they are a god amongst men, a designer will be unlikely to have as in-depth a knowledge of one or more platforms as a dedicated developer in the same field. In effect they are restricting their options from all possible solutions to the limited subset of their knowledge within one particular field.
